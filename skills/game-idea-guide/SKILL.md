# Game Idea Guide

Create coherent, playable game ideas that satisfy as many of the eight kinds of fun as possible: sensation, fellowship, challenge, fantasy, narrative, discovery, expression, and submission. For every idea, explicitly explain its fit with all eight and describe both an inner game loop and an outer game loop.

## Design priorities

- Aim to support all eight kinds of fun meaningfully. Identify the primary kinds that define the experience, then integrate the others wherever they strengthen the idea.
- Respect the user's audience, genre, platform, player count, development scope, and other constraints. Maximize meaningful coverage within those constraints; avoid adding unrelated systems merely to complete the checklist.
- Ground each claim in a concrete player action, mechanic, or experience. Explain what happens and why it produces that kind of fun; naming a category is insufficient.
- Identify limited or absent coverage honestly. Explain the gap and propose a compatible improvement when possible. Do not count a suggested future addition as an existing part of the concept.
- Prefer mechanics that support several kinds of fun together. Explain each connection separately instead of assuming that one feature automatically satisfies multiple categories.
- Base reasons to continue or return on enjoyable play, meaningful progress, mastery, curiosity, creativity, or shared goals. Explain the appeal beyond simply receiving more rewards.

## Apply the eight kinds of fun

### 1. Sensation

Design pleasure derived from the senses and from interacting with the game.

- **Touch:** Handling or collecting objects, moving board-game pieces, satisfying physical manipulation, or suitable controller haptics.
- **Auditory:** Music, rhythm, ambience, and responsive sound effects that create an emotional or physical response.
- **Visual:** Beauty, spectacle, animation, readable effects, satisfying reward feedback, and victory celebrations.

Specify a sensory moment tied to an action, such as a harvest producing a color burst and musical chime. Match the sensory channels to the platform; do not claim physical touch for an ordinary mouse click without explaining the experience.

Reference examples: Candy Crush for visual and auditory stimulation; board-game pieces and Warhammer miniatures for touch; Telltale games for visual storytelling.

### 2. Fellowship

Create enjoyment through players working together or sharing an experience.

Describe who interacts, what they do together, and how their interaction creates cooperation, belonging, trust, or shared enjoyment. Possible mechanisms include complementary roles, shared objectives, coordinated decisions, helping another player, and cooperative construction. Multiplayer support alone does not explain fellowship.

For a solo-only concept, identify fellowship as limited or absent unless a concrete shared-player experience fits the requested scope. Do not silently add online multiplayer or count friendly NPCs as player-to-player cooperation.

Reference examples: World of Warcraft guilds and raid teams; One Night Werewolf party play; Pandemic Legacy cooperation; basketball teamwork.

### 3. Challenge

Give players meaningful decisions and opportunities to develop mastery while overcoming obstacles.

Specify what skill improves, what decisions matter, how the game provides useful feedback, and how later situations test that mastery. Use timing, positioning, planning, deduction, resource tradeoffs, or other appropriate skills. Difficulty may come from adaptive opponents or deeper possibilities as well as staged escalation. Increasing enemy health or extending a grind alone does not establish meaningful challenge.

Reference examples: League of Legends character and strategic mastery; Street Fighter duels; basketball skill and decision-making.

### 4. Fantasy

Let players inhabit a role and act as someone in the game's world.

State the player fantasy and connect it to verbs, abilities, relationships, rules, and consequences. Explain what players can actually do that makes the role convincing; a setting description alone is insufficient.

Reference examples: inhabiting an adventurer's role in The Legend of Zelda; creating and role-playing a character in Dungeons & Dragons.

### 5. Narrative

Give players a story to experience as it develops.

Describe the situation, what changes, and how players encounter the next development through play. Use authored scenes, environmental storytelling, character relationships, player choices, or emergent events with understandable causes and consequences. Distinguish background lore from a developing story. Explain how narrative works with sensation and fantasy when relevant.

Reference examples: Telltale adaptations such as The Walking Dead and Guardians of the Galaxy; game adaptations such as Lego Star Wars.

### 6. Discovery

Reward curiosity through exploration, experimentation, and uncovering previously unknown things.

Specify what can be discovered, how players find it, and why it matters. Possibilities include hidden areas, quests, characters, resources, interactions, strategies, and world rules. Rewards can be knowledge, surprise, new options, story, or useful items. Explain the discovery process; a large map or a stream of unlocks alone is insufficient.

Reference examples: hidden areas in God of War; open exploration and self-directed routes in The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild.

### 7. Expression

Let players show creativity, individuality, or a preferred approach through meaningful choices.

Describe what players can customize, build, combine, perform, or decide, and how the result reflects their identity or style. Support distinct outcomes through construction, character presentation, builds, strategies, dialogue, or other suitable systems. Explain how choices differ in appearance, behavior, or meaning.

Reference examples: creating environments in Minecraft; experimenting with item builds and combinations in League of Legends.

### 8. Submission

Provide relaxation through satisfying activity with low effort or mental burden. Use "submission" in this sense of comfortable engagement and routine.

Specify an activity players can enjoy without constant difficult decisions, intense timing, or high stakes. Possibilities include familiar tending routines, collecting, gentle traversal, sorting, or low-pressure automation. Combine these with pleasant sensory feedback where appropriate. Low input alone does not guarantee relaxation if the activity remains stressful.

Balance challenge and submission through different activities, optional difficulty, or an alternating pace. Explain where players can relax and where they can seek mastery.

Reference examples: the simple press-to-play interaction of slot-machine games; planting and watering crops in Stardew Valley.

## Define both game loops

### Inner game loop: what players do during play

Describe the repeating activity that makes moment-to-moment play enjoyable. Write it as an explicit cycle using concrete verbs:

**Player action → Feedback or consequence → Immediate reward or changed state → Next decision or action → Repeat**

Identify the player's immediate goal, meaningful decision, feedback, and payoff. Explain what returns the player to the next repetition and, when useful, give an approximate cycle duration as a design assumption.

Using the supplied Vampire Survivors example, close the cycle as:

**Defeat enemies → Collect dropped XP and coins → Level up through XP and choose an upgrade → Use the improved build to defeat more enemies → Repeat**

Keep XP-driven progression during a run distinct from the coins that can feed the outer loop.

### Outer game loop: why players continue or return

Describe the larger cycle that gives repeated inner-loop play a continuing purpose. Write it as an explicit cycle:

**Complete a run, session, chapter, or milestone → Retain progress, knowledge, or an outcome → Choose a longer-term goal or change → Begin the next activity with new motivation or possibilities → Reach another milestone → Repeat**

State what carries forward, what resets if anything, what players decide between cycles, and how the next cycle changes. Explain both the longer-term goal and why pursuing it makes the next session appealing.

Using the supplied Vampire Survivors example:

**Run ends → Spend earned coins on persistent powerups → Start stronger → Pursue a further run milestone → Run ends → Repeat**

Adapt the outer loop to the genre. Persistent value can include new abilities, story progress, relationships, construction, ranking goals, player knowledge, or growing mastery. Do not require every game to have run resets, permanent stat upgrades, or an endless progression system. A finite story can sustain an outer loop until its resolution.

### Connect the loops

Explain both directions of the connection:

- **Inner to outer:** Name the resource, knowledge, relationship, or outcome produced during play that advances the larger goal.
- **Outer to inner:** Name how that advancement changes the next play cycle's decisions, capabilities, situations, or meaning.

Avoid describing the same action sequence twice at different lengths. The inner loop should explain the enjoyable repeated activity; the outer loop should explain its continuing purpose. "Play → Earn rewards → Repeat" is too vague for either loop.

## Generate or refine an idea

1. Use the user's stated constraints and requested number of ideas. If details are missing, make a few explicit, reasonable assumptions; ask only when an unresolved choice would materially change the concept.
2. Define the player role, the central activity, and a distinctive hook. Explain how the hook changes play rather than relying only on a genre mashup or unusual setting.
3. Build and connect the inner and outer loops. Establish what feels rewarding now and what creates a reason to continue later.
4. Map concrete mechanics to all eight kinds of fun. Identify primary strengths, weak coverage, and opportunities for one mechanic to serve several kinds.
5. Refine conflicts and scope. For example, place demanding challenges alongside relaxed preparation, or integrate narrative clues into discovery. Preserve explicit constraints when a category cannot fit.
6. Present each idea in the format below. For an existing idea, distinguish its current features from proposed improvements. For multiple ideas, provide both loops and all eight kinds of fun for each one; do not use a single combined assessment that hides gaps.

## Required response format

Adjust depth to the user's request while preserving the following content for every idea:

### Game title and hook

Give a short pitch that states the player role, main activity, and distinctive mechanic. Include genre, platform, player count, target audience, and session length when supplied or useful; label assumptions.

### Inner game loop

Provide an arrow cycle and briefly explain the immediate goal, decisions, feedback, and reward.

### Outer game loop

Provide an arrow cycle and briefly explain what persists, what resets if applicable, the longer-term goal, and what makes the next cycle appealing.

### How the loops connect

State what inner-loop play contributes to outer-loop progress and how that progress changes subsequent play.

### Eight kinds of fun

Provide an eight-row table with these columns:

| Kind of fun | Fit | Concrete mechanic or player experience | Why it creates this kind of fun |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

Include sensation, fellowship, challenge, fantasy, narrative, discovery, expression, and submission, in that order. Use **Strong**, **Supporting**, **Limited**, or **Absent** for fit. Treat these as design judgments, not measured player responses. For limited or absent coverage, explain the gap and any compatible improvement without presenting that improvement as already included.

### Design tradeoff

Identify the weakest fit or most important tension and explain the decision made to keep the idea coherent. When useful for a detailed concept, add the smallest playable prototype and the main question it should answer.

## Final quality check

Before delivering, confirm that:

- Every idea explicitly covers all eight kinds of fun with concrete explanations.
- Coverage is as broad as the concept and user constraints meaningfully allow; weak areas are acknowledged.
- The inner loop contains understandable player actions, feedback, and an enjoyable payoff.
- The outer loop explains what carries forward and why the player wants another cycle.
- Both loops close and influence one another without requiring every genre to imitate a roguelite.
- The pitch, mechanics, fun assessment, and progression describe the same feasible game.
