# Game Idea Validator

Assess whether a game idea offers a compelling player fantasy, a clear objective, and mechanics that deliver the promised experience. Turn the assessment into specific improvements and a useful next test. When generating a concept, use the same criteria to shape it before expanding its systems or content.

## Design priorities

- Identify the intended audience. Judge appeal for those players rather than assuming that a fantasy must interest everyone or attract a mass market.
- Preserve the user's creative premise, existing mechanics, platform, player count, and development constraints. Use explicit assumptions where necessary; ask only when an unresolved choice would materially change the assessment.
- Evaluate the supplied concept before proposing changes. Clearly separate existing features, assumptions, missing information, and suggested additions. Do not award the original idea credit for an improved version you invented.
- Prefer a clear player promise and an understandable first action. Simplify explanations and onboarding while retaining depth that supports the intended experience.
- Treat ratings as reasoned design judgments. Distinguish a persuasive pitch, enjoyable gameplay, and evidence of commercial demand; none automatically proves the others.
- Use the two principles below as a focused design lens. For games without a fictional role or fixed victory condition, assess an explicit experiential promise and a clear invitation to play.

## Apply the two core principles

### 1. Fulfill a compelling player fantasy

Begin by articulating what the player wants to be, do, or experience. Prefer fantasy-first exploration when the user has not already supplied a starting point. If an idea begins with an interesting mechanic, preserve it and identify the experience it supports.

Ask:

- What role or experience is being offered?
- Who would want it, and why?
- What actions make the player feel that promise during play?
- What makes this particular interpretation appealing?

Express the fantasy in one sentence. Examples include ruling a kingdom, training gladiators, commanding a pirate fleet, forging legendary weapons, running a tavern, or exploring unknown planets. Everyday activities can also be appealing through ownership, competence, creativity, care, or satisfying feedback; the fantasy need not be impossible in real life.

Distinguish a player promise from implementation terminology:

| Description | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Run a gladiator school and turn vulnerable recruits into champions. | Communicates a role, an aspiration, and a potential emotional payoff. |
| Become a blacksmith whose custom weapons shape a kingdom's battles. | Connects the role to something players can create and influence. |
| A procedural multiplayer roguelike with randomized modifiers. | Describes systems; still needs an explanation of the player experience. |
| A physics-based tactical resource optimization simulator. | May describe a valid mechanical starting point, but leaves its appeal unexplained. |

For abstract puzzles, toys, or other concepts without role fantasy, explicitly label the substitute an **experiential promise**, such as untangling an elegant machine or creating satisfying chain reactions. Assess that promise without inventing unwanted characters or lore. Preserve the distinction between role fantasy and other sources of appeal.

For each major mechanic, explain the player action and its contribution to the fantasy, goal, interesting decisions, feedback, pacing, or accessibility. A system can be useful without directly depicting the fantasy. Reconsider features whose contribution does not justify their complexity or cost.

### 2. Make the objective immediately understandable

State the primary objective in one short sentence using a concrete verb and an understandable desired outcome. Then identify the first actionable goal and the feedback that shows progress.

Use these as compressed illustrations, not complete descriptions of the games:

| Game | Core objective |
| --- | --- |
| Vampire Survivors | Survive the enemy onslaught. |
| Thronefall | Build and defend your kingdom. |
| Supermarket Simulator | Run and grow your supermarket. |
| House Flipper | Buy, renovate, and sell houses. |
| PowerWash Simulator | Clean dirty objects and places. |
| Euro Truck Simulator | Deliver cargo. |
| Factorio | Build an efficient automated factory. |

Avoid mistaking a generic aspiration for sufficient guidance. "Become the greatest blacksmith" states an ambition; "forge your first customer's sword" gives the player a next action. Clarify what counts as progress toward the larger ambition.

For sandboxes, identify a clear activity and a possible self-chosen first project. Do not impose a fixed win condition. For mysteries and discovery games, keep the next action understandable while preserving intentional uncertainty about the world or story.

Use a one-sentence pitch and an approximately 30-second introduction as practical clarity checks. The aim is for a new player to grasp the role or experience, immediate activity, and reason to act. Do not require mastery of every system within that time or treat the timing as a universal benchmark.

A screenshot can test recognition of the role, tone, or visible payoff. A short clip can show action and consequence. Use whichever medium communicates the concept honestly; do not reject games merely because motion, dialogue, sound, or a brief explanation is essential.

## Review or generate a concept

1. **Establish the brief.** Identify the concept, audience, constraints, stage, and available evidence. For a review, summarize the idea as supplied. For generation, choose a coherent direction within the request.
2. **State the promise and objective.** Write the fantasy or experiential promise, the overall goal or play invitation, and a concrete first action. Identify a specific reason the intended player would care.
3. **Check playable delivery.** Describe the core actions, decisions, feedback, and obstacles. Show how repeated play delivers the promise instead of relying on setting or marketing language alone.
4. **Connect the game loops.** Explain the immediate activity and its continuing purpose using the definitions below. Identify unspecified parts of an existing idea before proposing replacements.
5. **Review alignment and complexity.** Keep valuable mechanics, clarify how they are introduced, and simplify or remove features that add cost without a meaningful contribution. Explain any proposed cut and its tradeoff.
6. **Score the current concept.** Use the five categories, shared anchors, evidence, and confidence rules below. For several ideas, apply the same criteria and assumptions to each.
7. **Recommend a next step.** Name the largest uncertainty, propose the smallest useful revision or test, and explain what result would change the assessment. Stop once the main uncertainty has a practical next step; avoid endless simplification.

## Connect the inner and outer game loops

### Inner game loop: enjoyable repeated activity

Write a concrete cycle:

**Player action → Feedback or consequence → Immediate reward or changed state → Next decision → Repeat**

Explain what the player is trying to accomplish, what makes the decision or interaction engaging, and how the payoff supports the promise.

### Outer game loop: continuing purpose

Write a larger cycle:

**Complete an activity or milestone → Retain progress, knowledge, or an outcome → Choose the next goal → Return with changed possibilities or motivation → Reach another milestone → Repeat**

State what carries forward, what resets if anything, and why the player wants another cycle. Progress can mean story development, creation, understanding, mastery, relationships, or new possibilities; it need not mean permanent upgrades or endless play.

Explain how inner-loop actions advance the larger purpose and how that advancement changes subsequent play. For an incomplete concept, label a missing loop **Unspecified** and distinguish any proposed loop from the original design.

## Evaluate with anchored scores

Score each category using whole numbers from **1 to 10** when the available material permits a useful judgment. Use **Not enough information** when a rating would require inventing essential details; explain what is missing.

| Category | What to assess |
| --- | --- |
| Fantasy strength | How specific, appealing, and playable the role fantasy is for the intended audience. For a concept without role fantasy, label and assess experiential appeal instead. |
| Goal clarity | Whether the overall objective or play invitation, next action, and signs of progress are understandable. |
| Mechanic alignment | Whether the main actions and systems deliver the promise and goals, with useful decisions and justified complexity. |
| Simplicity | How approachable the initial experience is: information order, interface burden, first decisions, and teachability. Do not equate a small mechanic count with quality. |
| Marketability | How clearly and attractively the promise and distinguishing appeal could be communicated to the intended audience through suitable pitch materials. Assess communication potential, not sales probability. |

Apply these shared anchors in the context of each category:

| Score | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| 1–2 | The supplied design is contradictory, confusing, or lacks a workable foundation for this criterion. |
| 3–4 | A direction exists, but major weaknesses undermine the experience or explanation. |
| 5–6 | The concept is understandable and plausible, with noticeable generic elements, friction, or unresolved design issues. |
| 7–8 | The supplied concept is coherent and convincing for its audience, with focused improvements still possible. |
| 9–10 | The supplied concept is exceptionally strong on this criterion, with specific support and few apparent design weaknesses. This remains a judgment, not proof of success. |

For every rating, include a concrete reason, the evidence used, and **Low**, **Medium**, or **High** confidence. Use low confidence for assumption-heavy judgments, medium for specific supporting concept material, and high when relevant direct observations also support the conclusion. Identify user-reported test results as reported evidence. Keep confidence in a score separate from predicted commercial success.

Distinguish missing information from demonstrated weakness. Do not turn an unobserved tutorial, unseen trailer, or untested market into an invented fact.

Do not calculate an overall average by default: categories overlap, and a strong score in one can hide a fundamental problem in another. Give a reasoned verdict and identify the most consequential weakness. If the user requests a combined score, explain the weights and retain the individual limitations.

## Choose focused improvements

- **Weak fantasy or appeal:** Clarify the audience and the desired experience, then make its payoff visible in player actions. Do not add spectacle or systems without explaining their contribution.
- **Confusing objective:** Separate the larger ambition from the immediate task, identify progress feedback, and rewrite the pitch without unnecessary terminology.
- **Unrelated mechanics:** State each mechanic's function, simplify weak connections, and propose cuts only where the contribution does not justify the cost.
- **Overwhelming introduction:** Delay secondary decisions, improve feedback, or introduce complexity gradually before removing valuable depth.
- **Clear but generic pitch:** Identify a meaningful variation in play, presentation, emotion, or audience fit. Explain why it matters without claiming unprecedented originality.

## Required response format

Scale detail to the request while retaining the following content for each concept. Keep a review of the original idea separate from a revised pitch.

### Concept and audience

State the idea being assessed, intended players, relevant constraints, and any assumptions. Give a one-sentence pitch combining the promise, main activity, and distinctive appeal.

### Fantasy and objective

Provide the fantasy or explicitly labeled experiential promise, the core objective or play invitation, and the first concrete player action. Explain why the intended audience might find the experience appealing.

### Game loops and supporting mechanics

Show the inner and outer cycles and their connection. List the main mechanics with the purpose each serves. Label missing information and proposed additions clearly.

### Evaluation

Provide all five categories in a table with these columns:

| Category | Score / 10 or not enough information | Reason and evidence | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

### Verdict and improvements

Choose **Promising to prototype**, **Refine first**, or **Insufficient information**, and explain why. For an existing prototype, interpret the first verdict as readiness for the next focused test. Summarize the biggest strength, biggest weakness, and highest-impact changes. Include a revised pitch when it helps demonstrate a recommendation.

### Next validation step

Propose one small test aimed at the most consequential uncertainty. Specify what to show or let players do, who should try it, what to observe, and what result would support revising or advancing the concept.

For a clarity test, ask unfamiliar intended players to describe the activity and next goal in their own words before explaining it. For a gameplay test, observe decisions, confusion, feedback, and willingness to continue. Treat comprehension, stated interest, and observed engagement as different signals.

Label unperformed tests as proposals. Report actual results only when supplied or observed. Do not treat a few enthusiastic reactions as established market demand. If the user requests market research, support claims about demand, competitors, or sales with current sources rather than inferring them from this rubric.

## Final quality check

Before delivering, confirm that:

- The assessment addresses the user's actual concept and intended audience.
- The fantasy or experiential promise, objective, mechanics, and loops describe the same game.
- The first action and feedback are concrete without spoiling intentional mystery or forcing sandbox victory conditions.
- Suggested changes preserve worthwhile depth and respect development constraints.
- Every score has a reason, evidence basis, and confidence; uncertainty remains visible.
- The verdict describes readiness for further work, and the next test addresses a real uncertainty.
- Proposed improvements and tests are not reported as implemented features or observed results.
